## Part One: What You Actually Need at Closing and Through Month Three

The broker's $185,450 closing figure is incomplete in a way that matters. It captures your equity injection, your attorney, and the lender's fees, but it ignores the operating cash hole you walk into on day one because the seller is keeping the receivables and your largest parts supplier is putting you on COD. Let me build the real number from the ground up.

### Closing Day Cash Outflows

| Item | Amount | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Equity injection (10% of $1,540,000) | $154,000 | Your stated equity |
| Legal fees | $24,600 | Attorney's estimate |
| Lender fees | $6,850 | Term sheet figure |
| New lease deposit and first month | $23,400 | Two-month deposit + first month at $7,800 |
| **Closing day total** | **$208,850** | |

The lease item is the first number the broker missed. The deposit and first month are due when you sign the lease at closing, the landlord is the seller's own LLC, and there is no reason to expect flexibility there. That $23,400 is a hard closing day requirement.

### The COD Working Capital Gap: Months One Through Three

This is where the real exposure lives. The business currently runs on trade credit with its main parts supplier. That credit disappears at ownership transfer for 90 days. You have to pay for parts before you can bill them, and you won't collect cash on those jobs until 30 to 45 days after the work is done. That creates a float gap every single week.

To size this properly, start with revenue. At $2,837,400 projected annually, monthly revenue runs approximately $236,450. HVAC service companies typically carry parts and materials at 20 to 25 percent of revenue. Using 22 percent, monthly parts spend is roughly $52,000. Under normal trade terms of net 30, you carry about $52,000 of float at any moment. Under COD for 90 days, you have to fund that entire float yourself, and you have to fund it every month of the standby period before collections come back in.

Additionally, payroll for eleven people plus your own $120,000 salary runs approximately $82,000 to $88,000 per month in total labor cost depending on benefits. Payroll is not flexible. The first cycle hits before you have collected a dollar.

Here is a conservative cash flow bridge for the first three months:

| Month | Cash In (Collections, ~30 day lag) | Cash Out (Payroll + COD Parts + Rent + Debt Service) | Net Monthly | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Month 1 | ~$0 (no prior AR, seller kept it) | ~$148,000 | ($148,000) | ($148,000) |
| Month 2 | ~$196,000 (first month revenue collected) | ~$148,000 | $48,000 | ($100,000) |
| Month 3 | ~$236,000 | ~$148,000 | $88,000 | ($12,000) |

Month one is brutal because the seller retained $312,000 of receivables and you start from zero AR. Your collections in month one will reflect whatever jobs close in the first week or two of your ownership, not a full month of billings. The $148,000 monthly outflow uses $82,000 payroll plus taxes and benefits, $52,000 COD parts, $7,800 rent, and roughly $6,200 in debt service (the SBA payment starts immediately; the seller note is on standby). These are approximations and the actual number will move, but the direction and magnitude are reliable.

The cumulative shortfall bottoms around $148,000 in month one and recovers as your AR builds. You need enough working capital to cover that trough with a margin for error, unexpected repairs, a slow week, or a customer who pays late.

**Bottom line on cash needed:**

| Category | Amount |
|---|---|
| Closing day cash (equity + fees + lease) | $208,850 |
| Operating trough reserve, months 1–3 | $148,000 |
| Cushion for variability (10%) | $15,000 |
| **Total real cash requirement** | **~$371,850** |

## Part Three: The Working Capital Decision

The lender offered up to $150,000 of working capital on the same terms as the 7(a): 9.5 percent, ten-year amortization. If you take the full $150,000, here is what changes.

**On the closing cash side:** The working capital loan proceeds fund the operating gap rather than your personal savings. You close comfortably, retain the $40,000 savings buffer, and have approximately $12,150 of personal cash remaining above that buffer after the closing day outflows.

**On the debt service side:** Adding $150,000 at the same terms adds approximately $1,550 per month to your monthly debt service obligation, or $18,600 annually.

**The right answer is to take it, with two conditions.** First, structure the draw so the funds are available at closing but you only draw what you need as you need it, if the lender will permit a line structure rather than a term advance. Some SBA lenders will accommodate this; it reduces your interest cost on unused funds. If the lender insists on a full advance, take it anyway and hold the unneeded portion in a money market account earning 4.5 to 5 percent while you work through the COD period. Second, before you decide on the exact amount, have a direct conversation with the parts supplier about whether the COD requirement can be shortened. Some suppliers will cut it to 60 days with a personal guarantee or a modest deposit. If you get to 60 days COD instead of 90, your month-one cash requirement drops by roughly $30,000 to $40,000 and you might take $100,000 instead of the full $150,000.

Do not let the incremental monthly payment on the working capital loan deter you. The alternative is walking in undercapitalized, which creates execution risk that is worth far more than $1,550 a month to avoid.

## Part Four: Debt Service Coverage Analysis

### Building the Annual Debt Service Number

**SBA 7(a) loan amount:**
- 80% of $1,540,000 = $1,232,000
- Plus guaranty fee of $32,340
- **Total SBA loan: $1,264,340**

At 9.5 percent over ten years (120 months), the monthly payment on a standard amortizing loan is calculated as:

M = P × [r(1+r)^n] / [(1+r)^n – 1]

Where P = $1,264,340, r = 0.095/12 = 0.007917, n = 120

Monthly payment = $1,264,340 × [0.007917 × (1.007917)^120] / [(1.007917)^120 – 1]

(1.007917)^120 ≈ 2.5804

Monthly payment = $1,264,340 × [0.007917 × 2.5804] / [2.5804 – 1]
= $1,264,340 × [0.020425] / [1.5804]
= $1,264,340 × 0.012924
= **$16,340 per month / $196,080 annually**

**Working capital loan (if taken at $150,000):**

Same rate and term, monthly payment ≈ $1,550 per month / $18,600 annually.

**Seller note in Year One:**

The seller note is $154,000 (10% of $1,540,000) at 6 percent, on full standby for 24 months. During the standby, interest accrues and is added to the principal. No cash payments are due. For coverage purposes, the SBA and your lender will almost certainly exclude the seller note from the Year One DSCR calculation precisely because it is on standby. That is a standard SBA treatment for seller notes structured this way.

**Year One annual debt service (without working capital):** $196,080
**Year One annual debt service (with $150,000 working capital):** $214,680

### Year One Coverage

Adjusted EBITDA from the QofE: $361,400

This figure already reflects your salary, market rent, and the office replacement. That means it is essentially a proxy for pre-debt-service, pre-tax cash available to you. To be precise, you should subtract a normalized capital expenditure reserve for an HVAC fleet — typically $15,000 to $25,000 annually for a company this size — to get to true free cash flow for coverage. Use $20,000 as a midpoint.

**Adjusted EBITDA after maintenance capex:** $361,400 – $20,000 = $341,400

| Scenario | Cash Available | Annual Debt Service | DSCR |
|---|---|---|---|
| No working capital loan | $341,400 | $196,080 | **1.74x** |
| With $150,000 working capital | $341,400 | $214,680 | **1.59x** |

Both scenarios clear the lender's 1.25x threshold with meaningful room. Year One is not your coverage problem.

### Year Three Coverage: When the Seller Note Activates

After 24 months of standby, interest has accrued on $154,000 at 6 percent for two years. Simple interest accrual (most standby notes use simple accrual, though confirm this in the note documents) adds approximately $18,480, bringing the balance to roughly **$172,480**.

That balance then amortizes over five years at 6 percent:

Monthly payment = $172,480 × [0.005 × (1.005)^60] / [(1.005)^60 – 1]
(1.005)^60 ≈ 1.3489

Monthly payment = $172,480 × [0.005 × 1.3489] / [0.3489]
= $172,480 × [0.006745] / [0.3489]
= $172,480 × 0.019328
= **$3,332 per month / $39,984 annually**

**Year Three annual debt service (with working capital loan):**

| Component | Annual |
|---|---|
| SBA 7(a) | $196,080 |
| Working capital loan | $18,600 |
| Seller note (activated) | $39,984 |
| **Total** | **$254,664** |

**Year Three DSCR (assuming flat EBITDA):** $341,400 / $254,664 = **1.34x**

This still clears 1.25x, but the cushion has shrunk from 1.59x to 1.34x, and that assumes zero revenue or margin improvement over three years. If revenue grows at even 3 to 4 percent annually, which is modest for a healthy service company, Year Three EBITDA is closer to $375,000 to $385,000 and coverage stays comfortably above 1.4x.

The more important observation is this: **if the business does not grow and you have a down year in Year Three, you are closer to the edge than you would be with a less leveraged structure.** That is not a reason to kill the deal. It is a reason to manage the company's cash carefully through Years Two and Three and to build retained cash in the business during the standby period rather than taking distributions.

**Year Three DSCR (without working capital loan, same EBITDA):** $341,400 / $236,064 = **1.45x**

The working capital loan costs you about 11 basis points of coverage ratio in Year Three. That is cheap insurance for the liquidity it buys you in Year One.

## Part Five: What to Ask For Before You Sign

You have a gap to close and two coverage periods to protect. Here are the specific asks, ranked by importance and likelihood of success.

### Ask One: Seller Carries the Receivables Gap or Increases the Note (High Priority)

The cleanest solution to your working capital problem is to ask the seller to either (a) leave some receivables in the business rather than taking all $312,000, or (b) increase his seller note from $154,000 to $200,000 to $220,000, with the incremental amount designated as working capital. Option (b) requires lender approval and may not be possible under SBA standby rules. Option (a) is simpler and arguably fairer: the seller is walking away from a going concern and leaving you with zero AR while your largest supplier puts you on COD. Asking him to leave $80,000 to $100,000 of the most current receivables — the ones under 30 days — directly addresses your month-one cash problem without adding debt service.

Frame it this way to the seller: *"You kept $312,000 of AR and I start from zero while my supplier is on COD for 90 days. I need you to either leave me $100,000 of current AR or we revisit the price to reflect the working capital deficit I'm inheriting."*

### Ask Two: Supplier COD Period Reduction (High Priority, Zero Cost)

Call the supplier directly before the term sheet deadline. Ask whether a personal guarantee, a first payment by certified check, or a modest deposit — say $10,000 — will get you to 60-day COD instead of 90. Many suppliers will negotiate this with a buyer who is clearly capitalized and engaged. Cutting 30 days off the COD period is worth $40,000 to $50,000 in peak cash demand reduction.

### Ask Three: Take the Full $150,000 Working Capital (Already Decided)

Do this. The analysis above shows it clears coverage comfortably in both Year One and Year Three. The $1,550 monthly cost is far less than the operational risk of walking in thin.

### Ask Four: Request the Working Capital as a Line Rather Than a Term Advance

If the lender will structure the $150,000 as a revolving line or a delayed draw, you save interest on the portion you don't use during the COD period. Some SBA 7(a) lenders will do this; others won't because the SBA program has specific requirements for revolving credit. It costs you nothing to ask. Worst answer is no.

### Ask Five: Confirm the Seller Note Standby Terms in Writing Before Closing

The 24-month standby is valuable to your Year One cash flow, but make sure the note documents actually say what the term sheet implies. Specifically confirm: (a) is there a cash interest payment during standby or is it pure accrual, (b) is the accrual simple or compound, and (c) does the standby survive if you are in default on the SBA loan. Some seller note standby agreements have hidden trip wires. Have your attorney read this document before you sign the term sheet, not after.
