## The Core Issue: Annual RMDs Are Required During the 10-Year Period

The critical point is that because Lauren's father died **after his required beginning date** (he was 79 and had been taking RMDs for years), Lauren as a non-eligible designated beneficiary is not simply required to empty the account by December 31, 2032 and otherwise free to do nothing. Under the final SECURE Act regulations, she is required to take **annual RMDs in years 1 through 9**, calculated using her single life expectancy, *and* must empty the account by the end of year 10.

This matters enormously for the plan you've described.

---

## What That Means for 2023 and 2024

The IRS issued a series of notices (Notice 2022-53, Notice 2023-54, and Notice 2024-35) that waived the penalty for missed annual RMDs from inherited IRAs subject to this rule for **2021 through 2024**. So Lauren has not been penalized for skipping 2023 and 2024, and she doesn't need to make up those missed amounts. That relief was real and you were not wrong to set distributions aside while the guidance was unsettled. But that waiver period has now closed.

**2025 is the first year in which the penalty waiver no longer applies.** Distributions are required, and the excise tax for a missed RMD is 25 percent of the amount not taken (reducible to 10 percent if corrected promptly in a correction window, under the SECURE 2.0 changes).

## Reassessing the Drawdown Strategy

The broader plan—defer everything until retirement and draw down in 2030–2032—needs to be restructured. Here is a more accurate picture of what the obligation actually looks like:

**Years 2025–2029 (required annual RMDs, pre-retirement):** She must take RMDs each year regardless. At current account size those will likely run in the $30,000–$50,000 range annually, depending on account performance. These land in her 35 percent bracket, which isn't ideal but is unavoidable on the minimum required amounts.

**The question for planning purposes is whether to take *more* than the RMD in any of those years**, or whether voluntary distributions in 2030–2032 on top of the remaining required amounts create a manageable tax picture.

**Years 2030–2032 (post-retirement):** She still has annual RMD obligations in 2030 and 2031, and must fully empty the account by December 31, 2032. If the account has grown and she's deferred voluntary distributions, she could face a very large forced distribution in 2032. Depending on her other post-retirement income, that could push her into a higher bracket than she expects.

It may actually be worth modeling whether taking **voluntary distributions above the RMD in 2025–2029** at 35 percent is preferable to concentrating a large balance into 2030–2032, particularly if her post-retirement income isn't as low as anticipated (Social Security, pension, other investment income, etc.). Roth conversions from her own IRA, if she has one, might also interact with this in ways worth modeling.
