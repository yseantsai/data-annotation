I'm about to leave a regional operations job to buy a small HVAC service company outside Dayton, Ohio, and I need a second opinion on the broker's numbers before I sign a letter of intent. The broker wants the LOI by December 4. The company is Brightwater Mechanical, eleven employees, nine service vans, about 70 percent residential service and 30 percent light commercial maintenance contracts. The asking price is $1,980,000, which the broker's teaser describes as 3.2 times seller's discretionary earnings and "well below the 4 to 5 times EBITDA that HVAC service businesses trade for in this region."

Here is how the CIM gets to its $618,900 of adjusted SDE for 2025. Revenue was $2,837,400 and net income on the tax return was $214,500. The add-backs are the owner's salary of $165,000, the owner's health insurance and truck of $28,400, depreciation of $74,300, interest of $31,200, a legal settlement of $47,500 that the CIM labels non-recurring, and the owner's wife's salary of $58,000. She does the books and the dispatching, about thirty hours a week, and she is leaving with him.

A few things I noticed in the three-year P&L they sent. Legal and professional fees were $52,100 in 2023 and $39,800 in 2024. The company rents the shop from an LLC the seller owns for $4,200 a month, and his attorney already told mine that a new lease would be at market, which the broker's own comps put around $7,800 a month. The vans average seven years old, and the seller said on our walkthrough that three of them need replacing in the next year or so, at roughly $65,000 each. The biggest customer is a property management company at 34 percent of revenue on a maintenance contract that renews in March with 60 days' notice either way.

On my side: I would run the company full time and need to pay myself $120,000. A dispatcher and bookkeeper to replace his wife would run about $48,500 fully loaded, based on two quotes I got. The structure the lender sketched is a 10-year SBA 7(a) loan for 80 percent of the price at 10.25 percent, my 10 percent down, and a 10 percent seller note at 6 percent that sits on full standby for two years and then amortizes over five. The lender wants to see 1.25 times coverage after my salary. The deal is cash-free and debt-free, and the seller keeps the $312,000 of receivables.

Tell me whether this price holds up, what the cash flow really looks like after I pay myself and service the debt, whether the coverage test passes in the first year and again once the seller note starts paying, and what I should push back on in the LOI. If the right answer is to walk away or come in much lower, say so and show me the number you would offer.
