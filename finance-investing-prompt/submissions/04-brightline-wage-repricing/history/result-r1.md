# Brightline Building Services — 2027 Price Increase Analysis

## The Core Problem With the Current Framing

Before the numbers: the $320,000 figure you planned to present is overstated, and the reason matters. Dana's $925,000 revenue gain assumes a full year of pricing on your full book of business. But your contracts renew on staggered dates throughout the year, which means the new price only applies to each contract from its renewal date forward. A contract that renews July 1 only contributes half a year of the increase. On average across 143 contracts with start dates spread across the calendar, you'll collect roughly half a year of the pricing uplift in 2027. The wage increase, by contrast, hits on January 1 and runs for a full twelve months.

That timing mismatch is the central issue in this budget.

## Rebuilding the Numbers From the Ground Up

### Step 1: What the 6.5% Price Increase Actually Yields in 2027

Your $14,236,800 run rate × 6.5% = $925,392 is the full-year value of the increase — which matches Dana's figure. But with renewal dates spread evenly across the calendar, the average contract captures about six months of the new price in 2027. A reasonable estimate of the **cash-in revenue uplift** before churn is approximately:

**$925,392 × 50% = ~$462,700**

You could refine this if you have the actual renewal schedule. If your contracts cluster in Q1 (many commercial accounts renew in January), the number is higher. If they're truly uniform, 50% is the right assumption. I'd encourage you to pull the actual schedule before December 3 — this is the single biggest driver of whether the number holds.

### Step 2: Attrition

Dana expects to lose 4% of renewing revenue. With staggered renewals, accounts are churning throughout the year as they hit their renewal dates and face the new price. Lost accounts eliminate revenue, direct labor, and supplies together — so you need to think about this as lost **gross profit**, not just lost revenue.

**Revenue lost to attrition:**
$14,236,800 × 4% = $569,472 of annualized contract value lost. Because those cancellations also happen at renewal dates spread through the year, the in-year revenue loss is roughly half that:
**~$284,700 of 2027 revenue lost to churn**

**Cost savings from lost accounts** (labor + supplies leave with the account):
Your current gross margin on direct costs (labor + supplies) is:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| 2026 Revenue | $14,236,800 |
| Direct Labor (incl. taxes/comp) | $8,228,870 |
| Supplies & Equipment | $839,971 |
| **Total Direct Costs** | **$9,068,841** |
| **Gross Profit** | **$5,167,959** |
| **Gross Margin** | **36.3%** |

The direct cost ratio is 63.7%, so when you lose an account mid-year, you save roughly 63.7 cents of cost per dollar of lost revenue.

Cost savings from churned accounts: $284,700 × 63.7% = **~$181,400**
Net revenue/cost impact of attrition: –$284,700 revenue + $181,400 cost relief = **–$103,300 gross profit drag**

### Step 3: The Wage Increase Cost — Full Year

This is the one piece that is unambiguously a full-year number.

Your current direct labor before payroll taxes and comp is embedded in the $8,228,870. You've calculated the all-in wage increase cost at ~$604,000. I'll take that figure. If you want to verify it: the $1.35/hour raise is 7.34% on the base wage, and your grossed-up labor cost would increase by roughly that percentage, which on $8,228,870 yields ~$603,500 — consistent with your math.

**Full-year wage cost increase: –$604,000**

But here's the offset that's easy to miss: the accounts you lose to attrition were consuming labor at the old wage too. You need to net out the wage increase you *would have paid* on the lost accounts. The lost annualized labor on $284,700 of revenue (at the 57.8% labor-to-revenue ratio) × 7.34% raise ≈ roughly $12,100. That's a rounding-level adjustment but worth noting.

**Net wage increase cost: ~–$592,000** (after the labor that walks out with churned accounts)

## The Honest Answer to Your Question

**At 6.5%, you do not come out ahead in 2027. You likely give back roughly $230,000 of gross profit compared to 2026.** The owners need to know this is a bridge year. The increase is the right long-term move — you'll be fully priced at the new wage on 100% of your book by sometime in 2028 — but 2027 catches the full wage cost and only half the pricing benefit.

The $320,000 gain you planned to present will almost certainly become a loss when March actuals come in, which is exactly the outcome you're trying to avoid.

## What Rate Would Actually Hold 2026 Gross Profit?

To hold $5,167,959 of gross profit in 2027, you need the half-year pricing uplift net of attrition drag to cover the $592,000 wage increase.

Working backward:

You need net pricing benefit ≥ $592,000 + $103,300 attrition drag = ~$695,300

Half-year pricing uplift needed before attrition: ~$695,300 ÷ ~0.95 (attrition haircut on the revenue gain) ≈ $732,000

Full-year revenue uplift needed: $732,000 × 2 = $1,464,000

Required rate: $1,464,000 ÷ $14,236,800 = **~10.3%**

That's a materially different conversation. It also carries higher attrition risk — 4% assumed churn at 6.5% could easily be 7–9% at 10.3%, which would require iteration.

## What I'd Recommend You Present

**Option A — Honest framing of 6.5%:** Present it as a necessary investment in workforce retention with a 2027 gross profit impact of approximately –$225,000 to –$240,000, recovering to a full-year benefit of roughly +$320,000 in 2028 once the full book reprices. This is defensible and accurate.

**Option B — Staged approach:** Implement 6.5% now and flag in the budget presentation that a secondary adjustment may be needed at mid-year review if attrition runs below forecast and margin permits holding the line, or above forecast requiring a response.

**Option C — Higher rate with explicit analysis:** Present 8–9% as the range that gets you closer to neutral in 2027, with modeled attrition sensitivity. This requires Dana's input on what the market will bear and probably a client-by-client risk assessment on your largest accounts.

1. **Pull the actual renewal schedule.** If 40% of your revenue renews in Q1, the half-year assumption understates your 2027 benefit meaningfully. This one data point could shift the analysis by $80,000–$120,000.

If your largest five or ten accounts represent a disproportionate share of revenue, ask Dana what conversations she's had with them specifically. Concentration risk matters more than the average rate.

3. **Separate "gross profit impact" from "net income impact" in your presentation.** You haven't mentioned overhead — if you have supervisory or administrative costs that are partly variable, the gross profit figures above need a second layer. Make sure you're comparing the right line to the right line when you present to the owners.

The number you can stand behind in March is roughly a **$230,000 gross profit decline in 2027**, recovering to a **$320,000 improvement in 2028**. That's the defensible story, and it's also the one that demonstrates you've done the analysis correctly.
